# 02 · Access friction

The second axis, and the reason the map is worth building. Standing is public; **access friction is
almost never published**, which is exactly why assembling it creates something rankings cannot.

An undergraduate cannot benefit from excellence they cannot reach. A Tier 1 domain at friction 5 is
worth less to them than a Tier 2 domain at friction 1 — and no ranking will ever tell them that.

## The scale

| | Label | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Walk in | An open, rolling door with no selection. Volunteer programmes, public dig sites, a lab that publishes an application form. |
| **2** | Ask a PI | No formal process, but the group is small enough that a good email reaches a real person, and the work absorbs untrained hands. |
| **3** | Apply on campus | A formal programme with selection, but local and on your own campus. |
| **4** | Compete | Selective against a national pool, or gated behind something you must first acquire (a funded PI's allocation, a year of prior research). |
| **5** | Compete *and* relocate | National applicant pool **and** it is far enough away that term-time participation is impossible. |

The jump from 4 to 5 is distance, and it is the one students systematically underweight. Whitney at
1.5 hours and Scripps at 5 hours are both excellent; only one is a real option during term.

## How to research something nobody publishes

Standing comes from searching. Friction comes from **reading process pages like a lawyer and then
emailing**. In order of yield:

1. **Does a formal programme exist, and is its intake documented?** An REU with a published deadline
   is friction 3–4. A "students page" that describes a certificate but no intake mechanics is
   friction 3 *with a flag* — that gap is itself a finding.
2. **What must you already have?** HiPerGator is not self-service: every account needs a faculty
   sponsor with an existing allocation. That single sentence moves it from 2 to 4.
3. **Distance and term compatibility.** Get the actual travel time, not the mileage.
4. **When does the door open?** Annual, semester-boundary, or rolling. A Spring-only exchange needs
   a decision a year ahead — invisible unless you look at the terms column.
5. **Ask.** The remaining unknowns are answerable by one email. An unanswered question is a flag,
   not a guess.

## Record the doors, not just the number

The score is a sorting key; the **doors** are what a student acts on. For every entry list the
actual routes in — named programme, named contact, the deadline, and the fallback if the formal
route closes. "Cold-email an individual PI, there is no central intake" is a door. Say it plainly.

## Where the two axes interact

The most useful cell on the grid is **high standing, low friction** — and it is usually
underexploited precisely because it is unglamorous. At UF that was precision agriculture: the
best-ranked department in the university, with a formal REU, and students skip it to chase AI.

The second most useful is **high standing, high friction**, where the correct advice is not "apply"
but "plan a year ahead" — and saying so is more useful than listing it.
