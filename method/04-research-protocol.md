# 04 · Research protocol

How to gather, in what order, and when to stop.

## Source hierarchy

Rank every source before you use it. The same fact carries different weight depending on where it
came from:

1. **Independent primary** — an academy's own directory, a funder's award database, a competition's
   published results, a journal. Strongest.
2. **Reputable secondary** — trade press with editorial standards (C&EN, HPCwire, Nature news), or a
   student newspaper reporting a result it attended.
3. **The institution's own newsroom** — usable for facts (dates, appointments, dollar figures),
   **never** for superlatives. "$1.33B in expenditures" is fine. "Largest in the world" is a claim.
4. **A partner's marketing** — a hardware vendor's blog crediting the partnership it profits from.
   Cite only to note the conflict.
5. **Aggregators and directories** — often stale. A professor's page at a university they left five
   years ago will outrank their current one in search results. This bites, and it bit here.

## What to search for, in order

Standing first, because it determines whether the rest is worth researching:

1. **Academy membership** by name and unit — and check whether each is *sitting, emeritus, or dead*
2. **Major prizes** in the field, by name
3. **Named facilities**, then whether they are unique or merely large
4. **National programme roles** — and specifically **lead versus partner**
5. **Departmental rankings**, last, as corroboration rather than as evidence
6. **Then friction**: formal programmes, intake pages, deadlines, distances, term availability
7. **Then people**: provenance — where they trained, where they came from, where they went

## Coverage should be uneven, and declared

You will not research 200 people evenly. Prioritise by signal — academy members, directors, prize
winners — and then **say so in the artefact**:

> A missing entry means *not yet researched*, not *no connection exists*.

An undeclared gap reads as a claim of completeness. A declared one is an invitation to extend.

## Verify before you assert

Cheap checks that prevent expensive errors:

- **`curl` every URL before adding it.** Two of the Chinese university links were `http://`, which a
  guess would have got wrong. A dead link is worse than no link.
- **Search the person, not just the institution.** Stale index entries put people at universities
  they have left.
- **Check the year separately from the fact.** "Elected to NAS" and "elected in 2017" are two
  claims; the second fails re-confirmation far more often.
- **Distinguish lead from member** on every consortium, every time.

## When to stop

Stop when the next hour of searching would change a *tier* rather than add a *name*. Names are
endless; tiers are what the reader acts on.

Then write down what you did not check. The unchecked list is a real deliverable — it is what makes
the checked list believable, and it is the work plan for the next pass.
